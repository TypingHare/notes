\subsection{Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback}
\label{sec:rlhf}

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF)} replaces or supplements the environment reward with a learned model trained from human preferences~\cite{christiano2017deep}.

Humans can annotate an AI response by giving it a score that represents how good or bad the response is. We want to fine-tune a large language model (LLM) so that the model tends to generate responses with higher scores while decreasing the chance of generating responses with lower scores. However, having humans annotate AI responses can be inefficient. To address this, we can train a model to annotate responses based on human preferences.

First, we collect some high-quality \textbf{demonstrations}, which are example answers written by humans given a prompt \(x\). Then, for every prompt-response pair \((x, y)\), fine-tune a pre-trained language model with standard next-token prediction:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E} \left[ \sum_{t = 1}^{T}{ \log \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t}) } \right].
\end{equation}

This gives you a helpful initial policy (i.e., the fine-tuned model) \(\pi_{\text{SFT}}\), which should generate responses in a style similar to the demonstrations.

Next, we collect human preference data. For the same prompt \(x\), sample two responses from the fine-tuned LLM:
\[
	y_a \sim \pi_{\text{SFT}}(\cdot | x) \quad \text{and} \quad
	y_b \sim \pi_{\text{SFT}}(\cdot | x).
\]

A human annotator chooses a better response from \(y_a\) and \(y_b\). Let \(y_w\) and \(y_l\) denote the preferred response and less preferred response, respectively. A reward model \(r_\phi(x, y)\) maps a prompt-response pair \((x, y)\) to a scalar reward. A common preference modeling loss is the \textbf{Bradley-Terry / logistic loss}:
\begin{equation}
	L_{RM}(\phi) = - \mathbb{E} \left[ \log \sigma(\Delta r_\phi) \right],
\end{equation}

where
\begin{equation}
	\Delta r_\phi = r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l).
\end{equation}

Because humans prefer \(y_w\), it should be likely that \(\Delta r_\phi > 0\). The trained reward model should learn something like:
\begin{align*}
	 & r_\phi(\text{prompt, good answer}) = 4.2 \\
	 & r_\phi(\text{prompt, bad answer}) = -1.3
\end{align*}

Now, we want to optimize the language model policy \(\pi_\theta\) to maximize reward from the reward model. A simple objective would be:
\[
	\max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | x) } \left[ r_\phi(x, y) \right].
\]

In a nutshell, the classic RLHF pipeline for LLMs is
\OrderListBegin
\ListItem{
	\textbf{Supervised fine-tuning}: Train the model on high-quality demonstrations.
}
\ListItem{
	\textbf{Reward model training}: Train a reward model that learns which responses humans prefer.
}
\ListItem{
	\textbf{RL optimization}: The language model is updated to produce responses that score highly under the reward model.
}
\OrderListEnd

\vspace{1em}
\begin{NoteBox}{Real-world Example of RLHF}
	Imagine you are a literature major. In school, you are asked to read many classic and high-quality literary works, which serve as high-quality demonstrations. You learn from them and absorb them critically, cultivating your literary ability. This is similar to supervised fine-tuning.
	\medskip

	After you graduate, you start writing and publishing articles online. You receive feedback, or rewards, such as comments, views, and likes. Based on this feedback, you begin to understand the preferences of the public. This is similar to reward model training. After writing a new article, you are able to predict whether people will like it.
	\medskip

	Over time, you produce more and more popular articles by using your ability to tell which types of articles are likely to be liked by the public. This is similar to RL optimization.
\end{NoteBox}
